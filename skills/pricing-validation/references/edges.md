# The Edges — expanded guidance

For each edge: the framework behind it, what a PASS looks like, what a FAIL looks like, and how to correct. Use this when running a full validation; the summary table lives in `SKILL.md`.

A correction to one edge can break another, so after applying any fix, return to the sweep and re-check everything (see the iteration loop in SKILL.md).

---

## A — Demand & willingness to pay (Monetizing Innovation, rules 1–2)

### A1 — WTP grounding
**Why:** Ramanujam & Tacke's first rule is to have the willingness-to-pay conversation *before* you build the price, not after. A price set from cost or gut is a guess.
**Pass:** every price traces to a source of evidence: customer WTP research, a competitor's validated price point, or your own funnel (trial→paid, price-objection rate).
**Fail:** a number that exists only because it "feels right" or mirrors an old internal doc with no WTP basis.
**Correct:** attach a WTP source to each price. If none exists, mark NEEDS-DATA and research (competitor price points are the fastest proxy; a live no-card trial is the strongest signal).

### A2 — Segmentation by WTP, not demographics
**Why:** different customers pay different amounts for the same product based on *need*, not on who they are. Demographic tiers ("small / medium / large business") rarely track WTP.
**Pass:** each tier maps to one need-based persona you can name in a sentence, and the personas are distinct (no overlap that lets a buyer sit in two).
**Fail:** a tier whose buyer you cannot name, or two tiers competing for the same buyer.
**Correct:** redraw the tier boundary along the need/WTP fault line (e.g. depth of use, number of brands), not along company size.

## B — Packaging & value metric (Monetizing Innovation)

### B1 — Value metric
**Why:** the unit you charge on is the most strategic packaging choice. The ideal metric grows with the value the customer gets, is cost-defensible, and is simple to understand.
**Pass:** the metric (a) aligns with value received, (b) scales with the customer's success, (c) covers its own cost, (d) is understandable to a non-expert, and (e) does not corrode the brand promise.
**Fail:** a metric the customer rations against in a way that fights the brand (e.g. a "we handle it for you" promise undercut by a visible per-action counter), or one that decouples from cost.
**Correct:** if the metric fights the brand, change how it is *expressed* (generous ceiling, kept out of the moment of use) before changing the metric itself; if it decouples from cost, re-tie it.

### B2 — Leader / Filler / Killer placement
**Why:** features split into leaders (drive WTP and differentiate), fillers (necessary, neutral on WTP), and killers (repel a given segment). Where they sit determines whether the lineup makes sense.
**Pass:** the category-defining leaders are in the *entry* tier (so the entry product genuinely *is* the category, not a stripped version); killers are fenced away from the persona they repel; every upgrade is pulled by a leader the buyer can feel.
**Fail (hidden gem):** the single strongest differentiator is gated above entry, so the entry tier reads as a generic/cheaper competitor.
**Fail (killer in view):** enterprise/agency complexity shown to a solo buyer, making the product feel heavy.
**Correct:** move category-defining leaders down to entry; fence killers up; make sure each tier boundary is crossed because of a wanted leader, not an artificial wall.

### B3 — The four failures
**Why:** the book's taxonomy of how pricing/packaging dies.
- **Feature shock:** so many features/tiers that value is diluted and the buyer is confused. *Fix:* fewer, cleaner tiers; lead with outcomes.
- **Minivation:** a real innovation priced too low, leaving money on the table and under-signaling quality. *Fix:* raise to the WTP evidence; quality is signalled by price.
- **Hidden gem:** a would-be leader buried where no one sees or buys it. *Fix:* surface it (often: move it to entry, or make it the headline of its tier).
- **Undead:** shipping something customers do not actually want. *Fix:* cut it, or validate demand before pricing around it.
**Pass:** each of the four is explicitly cleared with a sentence of why.

### B4 — Fence integrity
**Why:** a fence only works if gaming it is not cheaper than the honest path.
**Pass:** the obvious gaming move (an agency buying several entry seats instead of the multi-brand tier; a power user living on entry forever) costs as much or more than buying the right tier, OR is blocked by what the right tier uniquely includes.
**Fail:** N entry seats are cheaper than the multi-brand tier and give the same value.
**Correct:** price the marginal unit (extra brand/seat) at roughly the entry price so splitting yields no gain, and make the higher tier include something the split cannot replicate (shared pool, admin, the leader features).

## C — Psychology & positioning coherence (Cialdini + Dunford + Helmer + StoryBrand)

### C1 — Anchor & decoy
**Why:** buyers judge prices relative to references. A high anchor makes the target look reasonable; a center-stage "most popular" nudges choice; loss aversion (experienced in a trial) pulls upgrades.
**Pass:** there is a clear high anchor (a top tier or a "contact us"), an honest target tier, and ratios/gaps that create pull without a fake badge.
**Fail:** a "most popular" badge on a tier that will not be most popular (reads as manipulation, and customers feel it); or no anchor, so every tier looks expensive in isolation.
**Correct:** badge by truth; let the trial expose the target tier so loss aversion does the honest pulling; if the multi-brand tier cannot be a high anchor (because anti-split caps it low), let a "contact us" tier carry the anchor.

### C2 — Price-as-signal
**Why:** price is read as a quality and category signal before the product is used (Dunford: the category sets the price reference; Helmer: brand power realizes as a premium).
**Pass:** the entry price puts the product in the intended category (e.g. a "partner," not a "toy tool"), and the price coheres with the stated positioning and moat.
**Fail:** a premium, relationship-led positioning priced in throwaway-tool territory; the price contradicts the story.
**Correct:** raise the floor out of the contradicting band, or change the positioning — but do not ship a price that argues against your own category.

### C3 — Pricing strategy chosen, not defaulted
**Why:** skim (high, capture WTP, lower later), penetration (low, buy share), and neutral are different bets with different implications.
**Pass:** the choice is stated and justified against brand power, the capital/funding plan, and the go-to-market motion.
**Fail:** a price that is low "to be safe" without acknowledging it is a penetration bet a bootstrap may not afford, or high without acknowledging the proof-of-value burden.
**Correct:** name the strategy and reconcile it with the moat and the capital plan.

### C4 — Value story
**Why:** Monetizing Innovation's last rule, every unit of price justified in value; StoryBrand, anchor on the transformation and the alternative.
**Pass:** one sentence that a skeptical buyer hears as obviously worth it, anchored on the real alternative ("a [partner] for $X/day instead of a [$Y/mo agency]").
**Fail:** a justification that leans on features, or on being cheaper than a rival you claim to be better than.
**Correct:** re-anchor on the expensive alternative the buyer is escaping, and on the transformation, never on attacking a rival's quality.

## D — Economic reality (the cost model)

### D1 — Margin closes
**Why:** a plan that does not survive its own usage is not a plan.
**Pass:** build margin from the real cost model at three points — typical usage, at the stated limit, and at the worst case (e.g. the expensive model/path or the heavy-usage tail) — and confirm margin clears the declared floor at each, both with and without any temporary subsidy (credits, etc.).
**Fail:** margin only computed at "typical"; the limit or worst-case quietly goes underwater.
**Correct:** adjust the limit, the price, or the metric expression until the worst case clears the floor; if a subsidy is the only thing holding it, state the post-subsidy plan explicitly.

### D2 — Internal reconciliation
**Why:** in an interlocking system, two cells can silently contradict.
**Pass:** ladder ratios are sane, anti-split holds, per-unit costs/prices are consistent across tiers, and the limit gradient rises sensibly tier to tier.
**Fail:** the multi-brand tier gives *less* per-brand headroom than a single mid tier; a marginal-unit price that breaks anti-split; a gradient that inverts.
**Correct:** walk every number against every other and fix the contradiction; re-sweep, because the fix usually moves another cell.

## E — Competitive & experience (Dunford + behavioral)

### E1 — Value-per-dollar vs the real alternative
**Why:** buyers compare, even across categories. The job is not to deny the comparison but to win it on your axis.
**Pass:** at each tier, when the buyer puts you next to the alternative they actually weigh, you win value-per-dollar on the dimension you compete on; and no single counter-example breaks the claim.
**Fail:** a comparison that loses on volume/price once units are normalized, or a claim a single good competitor output disproves ("their content is generic").
**Correct:** normalize the units and compare like-for-like; move the comparison to your durable axis (relationship, depth, fit); research the competitor's real offering before asserting.

### E2 — Customer feeling at each touchpoint
**Why:** pricing is experienced, not just calculated. The most loyal users are often the heaviest, and a fence that bites them at their most-engaged moment breeds resentment.
**Pass:** walking the page, the limit, the upsell, the entry experience, and the multi-brand experience, none produces anxiety, a stripped-down feeling, or a sense of being manipulated.
**Fail:** a counter ticking down while the user is in the creative act; an entry tier that feels like bait; a badge that feels like a trick.
**Correct:** move the meter out of the moment of use; make sure entry feels like the real product; badge honestly; design the limit so it bites only the genuine outlier, then converts them upward rather than punishing them.
